Photo locker app for Android: 6 tests before you trust one

Search for a photo locker on Google Play and you get hundreds of apps with padlock icons and near-identical promises. Some protect your photos well. Some only move them to a folder and put a PIN screen in front. These six tests tell them apart in about ten minutes, before you hand over anything private.

Quick answer

A good photo locker app gives your photos and videos their own lock, encrypts the files on the phone so they can't be opened outside the app, works without an account or internet, and gives you a recovery method plus backups you control. Test any locker with a dummy photo first: look for it with a file manager, check it opens offline, read its Data safety section, and make sure you can get back in and restore a backup.

What a photo locker is (and what it isn't)

“Photo locker” covers a few different kinds of product, so it helps to be clear which one you want.

  • Locker apps on Android. Apps that store your pictures and clips behind a pattern, PIN or fingerprint. This guide is about these. People also call them photo vaults, gallery lockers or photo and video lockers.
  • Built-in private albums. Google Photos Locked Folder, Samsung Secure Folder and private albums from Xiaomi, OPPO, realme and others. Free, but each has limits, covered below.
  • Physical photo lockers. If you searched “photo locker amazon” looking for a box to store printed photos, that's a different thing. Acid-free storage boxes are what you want there.

Among locker apps, the important split is between apps that hide files and apps that encrypt them. A hiding app moves your photo to an obscure folder, often with a dot at the start of the name or a .nomedia file so the gallery skips it. The photo itself is unchanged. Anyone who finds that folder can open it. An encrypting app scrambles the file, so the copy on the phone's storage is useless without your key. App listings rarely say which one they are in plain words, which is why testing matters.

The 6 tests to run on any photo locker

Use a throwaway picture for all of these, such as a photo of a coffee cup. Don't put anything real in until the app has passed.

  1. The file manager test. Lock the test photo, then open a file manager that can show hidden files and browse the phone's storage. If you can find a folder with your coffee cup in it and it opens as a normal picture, the locker only hides files. If you find only files with odd names that won't open as images, that's a good sign the files are encrypted.
  2. The airplane mode test. Turn on airplane mode, close the app fully and open it again. A locker that refuses to open, or nags you to sign in, depends on a server. Your private photos shouldn't need one.
  3. The Data safety test. On the app's Google Play page, open Data safety. Look at “Data collected” and “Data shared”. Photos and videos, device IDs, location or contact details listed there all leave the phone. Google says it does not verify these answers, so treat them as the developer's own claims, and check the next test too.
  4. The permissions test. On the same page, under About this app, look at the permissions. A locker that has network access can send data out, whatever its policy says. One without it can't.
  5. The forgot-code test. Find out exactly what happens if you forget your lock. An email reset means there is an account, and usually that the company can let someone in. A recovery phrase or code that only you hold is the safer design, as long as you write it down.
  6. The restore test. Make a backup, then restore it, ideally on another phone or after reinstalling. A locker whose files vanish when you uninstall it, clear its data or switch phones is a trap. You want encrypted backups you can copy somewhere yourself.

Most apps fail at least one of these. That doesn't always rule them out, but now you know the real risk you're taking.

Photo and video lockers: extra things to check

Videos are where many lockers struggle. They're large, and an app has to decrypt them on the fly to play them.

  • Free limits. Free tiers often allow far fewer videos than photos. Check the numbers before you start moving a big collection.
  • Playback. Lock a long clip and scrub through it. Stutter or long waits suggest the app copies the whole video out before playing, which can leave a temporary unencrypted copy behind.
  • Camera copies. If the locker can take photos or videos itself, check your gallery afterwards. Some camera apps keep their own copy.
  • Originals. Moving a video into a locker doesn't always delete the original. Check the gallery, the trash, Google Photos and WhatsApp folders.

Calculator photo lockers: useful, with one caveat

A calculator photo locker looks like a normal calculator on your home screen. It does real sums, and a secret code opens the hidden photos. This helps when the risk is someone scrolling your app list and asking “what's that vault app?”.

The caveat: the disguise only hides the app. It says nothing about the files behind it. Plenty of calculator lockers store photos in a plain hidden folder, so they fail the file manager test above. A disguise is a good extra layer on top of encryption, not a substitute for it. We compare several in Sealcloak vs Calculator Vault.

Mistakes people make with photo lockers

  • Moving real photos in on day one. Test with a dummy photo first. Some apps lose files on an update or a reinstall.
  • Using the phone's PIN for the locker. If others know your screen lock, a locker with the same code protects nothing.
  • Leaving the originals. The gallery trash keeps deleted photos for weeks on many phones. Empty it after moving files.
  • Not saving the recovery details. Lockers that truly encrypt can't be reset by the developer. That's the point, but it means the phrase or code is your only spare key.
  • Uninstalling without exporting. Unlock, export or back up first. Assume anything left inside is gone.
  • Picking by star rating alone. Ratings reward looks and ease, not whether your files are encrypted. Read the one-star reviews for lost files and forced sign-ins.

Bottom line

The best photo locker is the one that passes the tests that matter to you. For light privacy, Google Photos Locked Folder or your phone's private album is free and fine. If others know your phone PIN, or the photos would cause real harm in the wrong hands, choose a locker with its own lock, real file encryption, no account, and backups you hold.
