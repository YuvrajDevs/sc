My partner wants my phone password

It might be a casual question, a request after an argument, or something that happens every evening. Wanting to know what's on your partner's phone is common; feeling you have no say in it isn't okay. Here's how to think it through, talk about it, and keep the things that are yours private, calmly.

What to do now

Notice how the request feels: curious, anxious or controlling. Talk about privacy at a calm moment, and agree boundaries you're both comfortable with. Keep private photos and files behind a separate lock that isn't your phone's screen lock. If you feel afraid, checked on constantly or punished for privacy, contact a domestic abuse helpline for confidential advice.

Privacy isn't the same as secrecy

Everyone has things that are private without being wrong: messages from friends who trusted you, photos of your body, medical information, things from before the relationship, half-formed thoughts in notes. Wanting those to stay yours doesn't mean you're hiding something. Healthy relationships have room for both closeness and privacy.

At the same time, a partner's request often comes from a real feeling: insecurity, a past betrayal, or anxiety. Understanding where it comes from helps you respond kindly without giving up your privacy.

How to talk about it

  1. Pick a calm moment, not in the middle of an argument.
  2. Start with the feeling, not the phone: "I want you to feel secure with me. Can we talk about what's worrying you?"
  3. Explain privacy plainly: "There are things on my phone that belong to other people, or that are just mine. That isn't about you."
  4. Agree on boundaries that you both choose, for example: emergency access yes, reading messages no.
  5. Revisit it if things change. Agreements can be updated.

Other everyday protections

  • Change your screen lock if it's been shared or watched, and remove fingerprints or faces you didn't add yourself.
  • Hide message previews on the lock screen in your notification settings.
  • Check WhatsApp's Linked devices and your Google account's device list for sessions you don't recognise.
  • Look at signs someone went through your phone.

When it's more than worry

Demanding passwords, checking your phone in secret, tracking your location, reading your messages, or getting angry when you want privacy can all be part of controlling behaviour. If you feel watched, afraid or not free to say no, that's not an overreaction. Domestic abuse helplines offer confidential advice, including how to secure your phone safely; sudden changes can sometimes increase risk, so they can help you plan. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.
