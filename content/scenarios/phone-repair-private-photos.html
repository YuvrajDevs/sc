Giving your phone for repair: how to protect private photos

A cracked screen or a dead charging port means handing your whole life to a stranger for a day or two: photos, chats, banking apps and email. Most repair technicians are honest, but you shouldn't have to rely on it. Fifteen minutes of preparation keeps your private things private and makes sure nothing is lost if the phone gets reset.

What to do now

Back up your phone, including any vault app. If your phone has a repair or maintenance mode, turn it on before you hand it over; it hides your data and apps behind a separate profile. Otherwise, move private photos into an encrypted vault, sign out of sensitive apps, and take out the SIM and memory card.

Before you go: the checklist

  1. Back up first. Repairs can end in a reset, especially board or software work. Back up your photos (Google Photos, a computer or an SD card) and make a fresh backup of any vault app. In Sealcloak that's an encrypted backup file; copy it off the phone.
  2. Check for repair mode. Recent Samsung Galaxy phones have Maintenance mode (look in Settings under Battery and device care). Recent Google Pixel phones have Repair mode (under System settings). Both restart the phone into a separate profile so the technician can test it without seeing your apps, photos or accounts. Turning it off brings everything back.
  3. Lock what stays. If there's no repair mode, move private photos, videos and documents into an encrypted vault and delete them from the gallery and its trash.
  4. Sign out of the most sensitive apps: banking, payment apps and email on phones without repair mode.
  5. Remove the SIM and memory card. The shop doesn't need them, and the SIM can receive your login codes.
  6. Note the phone's condition with a photo of it, and write down the IMEI (dial *#06#).

What the technician can see

If the phone is unlocked, a technician can open the gallery, messaging apps, files, email and anything else you can. That includes things you might forget about: WhatsApp's media folder, screenshots, downloaded PDFs of ID cards, and the gallery's recently deleted folder, which keeps pictures for weeks on many phones.

If the screen is broken and they only replace it, they may never unlock the phone at all. Ask whether they need your lock code. For a screen, battery or port, the honest answer is often no; they can test touch and charging on the lock screen.

Why an encrypted vault helps here

Hiding a photo in the gallery's hidden album or a "hidden folder" only keeps it off the main screen. Anyone holding the unlocked phone can open those in seconds. An encrypted vault is different: the files are scrambled, and only your vault's own pattern turns them back into photos.

In Sealcloak, each vault has its own pattern, separate from your phone's screen lock, so giving a technician your screen PIN doesn't open it. Screenshots are blocked inside the app, and with the calculator disguise the app looks like an ordinary calculator. After four wrong patterns, it makes the next attempts wait longer and longer.

Choosing a repair shop

Where you take the phone matters as much as how you prepare it. Authorised service centres for your brand usually have written data policies and often repair while you wait or in a separate secure area. Independent shops can be excellent and cheaper; pick one with reviews that mention honesty, and ask a few questions before you leave the phone:

  • Do you need my screen lock for this repair?
  • Could this repair reset the phone or erase data?
  • Can I wait while you do it, or watch the testing?
  • Who will have access to the phone while it's here?

For a quick screen or battery swap, waiting in the shop is the simplest privacy protection of all. Ask for an itemised receipt that lists the parts replaced, too.

When the phone comes back

  1. Turn off repair or maintenance mode and check everything is there.
  2. Open your Google account's security page and look at recent activity and devices.
  3. Check your banking and email apps for unexpected logins.
  4. If you shared your screen lock, change it.
  5. Put your SIM and memory card back.

If something feels wrong

If you think someone copied your photos or accessed accounts while the phone was away, change your passwords from another device, sign out of all sessions in your Google account, and keep the repair receipt. If private images are misused or someone threatens you with them, contact the police; many countries also have support lines for image-based abuse.
