Kids using your phone: how to hide photos from kids

You hand over your phone for one cartoon, and five minutes later your child has swiped into the gallery, sent a voice note to your boss and found photos you'd rather they didn't. It happens to every parent, grandparent, aunt and uncle. A few settings stop almost all of it, without making the phone a hassle for you.

What to do now

Turn on app pinning in Settings, then pin the video or game app before you hand the phone over; your child can't leave it without your lock. Move private photos out of the gallery into an encrypted vault and empty the gallery's trash. Turn on purchase authentication in the Play Store.

Pin the app: the single most useful setting

App pinning keeps the phone locked to one app. Children can play or watch, but they can't swipe home, open the gallery or reach your messages. It's built into Android, and few people know it's there.

  1. Open Settings and search for pinning. It's usually under Security, sometimes in Advanced or Other security settings. Samsung calls it Pin windows.
  2. Turn it on, and turn on the option to ask for your PIN or pattern before unpinning.
  3. Open the app your child will use, then open recent apps, tap the app's icon at the top of its card and choose Pin.
  4. To unpin, hold Back and Overview (or swipe up and hold, depending on your navigation), then unlock.

It takes a minute to set up once, and a few seconds each time after that.

Where children stumble on photos

Even with pinning, there are moments you forget. These are the places kids land most:

  • The gallery and camera. Opening the camera then tapping the thumbnail shows your latest photos, even on some lock screens.
  • WhatsApp and other chats, with their own photo galleries.
  • Google Photos memories and widgets that put old photos on the home screen.
  • The gallery's trash, which keeps deleted pictures for weeks.
  • The share sheet: one tap and a photo is sent to a recent contact.

Turning off memories or photo widgets on the home screen, and keeping private photos out of the gallery altogether, handles most of it.

Grandparents, babysitters and shared family phones

The same setup works when the phone isn't yours alone. If grandparents or a babysitter hand a phone to the children, show them how pinning works once, and write the steps on a note. On a shared family phone, keeping each adult's private files in their own vault means nobody has to police what anyone else stores. Sealcloak allows up to three vaults, each with its own pattern, and nothing in the app shows how many exist.

Stop accidental purchases and messages

  1. In the Play Store, open your profile, then Settings, then Authentication, and require it for all purchases.
  2. Turn off one-tap payments in apps and games that offer them.
  3. In YouTube, use YouTube Kids for younger children; it has its own content settings.
  4. Lock your messaging apps with fingerprint if they offer it; WhatsApp does under Privacy.

As they get older

Pinning works brilliantly for toddlers and is frustrating for a ten-year-old who wants to switch between apps. At that point, a kids mode or separate user profile (on phones and tablets that offer them), or a device of their own managed with Google Family Link, gives them more freedom and keeps your data out of reach. A cheap tablet set up just for them often saves a lot of worry.

It's also a good moment to teach them about privacy: that photos of other people belong to those people, and that it's normal to have things on a phone that are private.
